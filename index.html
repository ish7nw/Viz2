Arrivals, origins and settlement, 1788–2025

Overview01 Over time02 Arrival03 Origins04 Settlement05 AgeMethodsReferences

Almost one in three people living in Australia was born overseas (Figure 1). At 30 June 2025, 8.8 million of the 27.6 million residents were born in another country. The largest groups came from South and Central Asia, led by India, and from North-West Europe, led by England. The sections below trace how that came about: when people arrived, where they came from, where they settled and how old they are.

Who
Anyone curious about how migration has shaped Australia, such as students, teachers and general readers. No statistics background is needed.
What
Country of birth, year of arrival, language and age from the ABS Census (1891–2021) and estimated resident population (1996–2025), mapped on ABS and Natural Earth boundaries. See Notes on data and method.
Why
To show where Australia’s migrants come from, how that has changed since 1788, and where they live today.
How
Twenty charts in five sections, from the whole country down to suburbs. Maps of the same measure share colour classes, the Settlement maps are linked by state, and every chart gives exact figures on hover.

01Migration over time

Figure 2 shows the overseas-born share of the population from the first country-of-birth records in 1891. Three phases stand out: a long decline from 1891 to 1947, through Federation, the Immigration Restriction Act, two world wars and the Depression; a steep rise after 1947 under the post-war migration program; and a second rise from 2000, interrupted briefly by the COVID-19 border closure in 2020–21.

Figure 3 counts the people living in Australia in 2021 by when they arrived and where they were born. Arrivals of the 1950s were overwhelmingly European: at least 80% of those still living here who arrived in 1951–60 were born in Europe, and in the 1960s the United Kingdom and Ireland alone made up 45%. In the 1980s South-East Asia was the largest source. From 2001 South and Central Asia led, and in 2018 it made up more than a third of arrivals. The narrowing at the right edge is the COVID-19 border closure of 2020–21.

02Arrival

The First Fleet of eleven ships left Portsmouth on 13 May 1787 with more than 1,500 people and reached Botany Bay in January 1788, after stops at Tenerife, Rio de Janeiro and Cape Town (Figure 4). About 162,000 convicts were transported to Australia between 1788 and 1868.

03Countries of origin

3.1Where residents were born

Figure 5 places the ten largest overseas birthplaces on a map centred on Sydney. Rings mark equal distance from Sydney every 2,500 km, so each country can be read against how far it is from Australia. New Zealand is the nearest large source at about 2,200 km. India and China lie at 9,000–10,000 km, and England lies beyond 15,000 km.

3.2Flows

Figure 6 draws a line from each birthplace to Australia, with line width proportional to the number of residents in 2025. The three widest flows, from India, England and China, together account for 2.67 million people, about 30% of the overseas-born population.

3.3Change, 2015–2025

Between 2015 and 2025 the India-born population grew by 522,000, the largest increase of any country, followed by China (223,000), the Philippines (171,000) and Nepal (163,000). England is the only top-ten birthplace that declined, by about 36,000, reflecting the ageing of the post-war cohort (Figure 7).

3.4Rank over time

The order of birthplaces has changed more in thirty years than the totals suggest (Figure 8). In 1996 Italy, Scotland, Greece, Germany and the Netherlands were all in the top ten; by 2025 only England remained from Europe. India rose from twelfth to first, passing England in 2025 by fewer than 100 people in preliminary estimates. China rose from eighth to third, and by 2025 Nepal was in the top ten.

4.1Share born overseas by region

Below the state level, the overseas-born population is concentrated in the capital cities (Figure 9). In 2021, 34.4% of residents of the Greater Capital City areas were born overseas, against 14.5% in the rest of the country. Sydney – Parramatta had the highest share of the 88 SA4 regions, 52.2%, followed by Sydney – Ryde (48.0%) and Sydney – South West (47.4%). The lowest shares were in inland New South Wales, led by Far West and Orana at 7.3%. Outside the capitals, only the Gold Coast came close to the national figure, at 28.9%.

Figure 9 makes the large, thinly populated regions dominate. Figure 10 gives every SA4 region a hexagon of the same size, so the 46 regions in the capital cities get the same space per region as the 42 outside them. The dark cluster of Sydney and Melbourne regions, small on the map, becomes the centre of the picture.

Figure 11 puts the same 88 regions on a single scale, one row per state. In every state the capital-city regions (filled dots) mostly sit to the right of the rest of the state. The spread is widest in New South Wales, where Sydney’s regions run from 16.1% (Central Coast) to 52.2% (Parramatta) while the rest of the state stays below 20%. Queensland is the exception that overlaps: the Gold Coast, at 28.9%, is above several Brisbane regions.

Zooming in to suburbs shows how uneven this is inside each city (Figure 12). Of the 1,400 capital-city SA2 areas with at least 300 residents, 161 had a majority born overseas: 80 in Sydney, 60 in Melbourne, 11 in Brisbane, 7 in Perth, 2 in Canberra and 1 in Adelaide. The highest shares were in Haymarket in central Sydney (76.9%) and the north of the Melbourne CBD (75.4%), followed by Auburn – North and Parramatta – North (both 75.3%). In Sydney the high shares run west from the city centre through Burwood and Strathfield to Parramatta and Auburn. Hobart’s highest share, in Sandy Bay, was 35.9%.

4.2Level and growth

Figure 13 combines the 2021 share with its change since 2011. Fifteen regions are high on both measures, all of them in the capital cities: eight in Sydney, four in Melbourne, two in Brisbane and one in Perth. Sydney – Ryde grew the most, by 7.9 percentage points, to 48.0%. Most of regional Australia is low on both measures, and in six regions the share did not rise at all, including Illawarra and the Mornington Peninsula.

4.3Where overseas-born residents live

Figures 14 to 16 show the same 2021 totals in three ways. The dot density map shows concentration in the south-east. The cartogram resizes each state by its overseas-born population, and the pie charts compare overseas-born and Australian-born residents within each state.

4.4Largest communities

The largest overseas birthplace differs by state (Figure 17): China in New South Wales, India in Victoria and the ACT, New Zealand in Queensland, the Philippines in the Northern Territory, and England in Western Australia, South Australia and Tasmania. England is the largest group in three states, India in two, and China, New Zealand and the Philippines in one each. Hovering one map highlights the matching places on the other.

4.5Where in India and China

The Census records country of birth but not the state or province, so Figure 18 uses the language people speak at home to look inside India and China. Mandarin, the national language of China, is the most common of these languages in every state, at around 34 speakers per 1,000 residents in Victoria and New South Wales. Cantonese, from Guangdong, Hong Kong and Macau, is most concentrated in New South Wales (18.4 per 1,000). Among Indian languages, Punjabi is strongest in Victoria (16.1) and South Australia (11.2), Hindi in the ACT, Victoria and New South Wales (about 10 each), Gujarati in South Australia (4.2), and Malayalam, from Kerala, in the Northern Territory (6.8).

4.6Languages from around the world

Figure 19 extends the comparison to 34 languages, including those of Figure 18. After Mandarin, the most widely spoken are Arabic, Vietnamese and Cantonese. Each has a home state: Arabic in New South Wales (28.1 per 1,000 residents), Vietnamese and Greek in Victoria (18.3 and 16.5), and Filipino or Tagalog in the Northern Territory (22.1). Smaller communities show the same pattern. Nepali is highest in the Northern Territory (13.3) and Tasmania (13.0), Afrikaans in Western Australia (5.5), and Samoan in Queensland (3.2).

05Age

Migrants usually arrive as young adults, so the overseas-born population has few children and a bulge in the working ages (Figure 20). In 2025, 38.6% of overseas-born residents were aged 25–44, against 24.6% of the Australian-born, and only 4.8% were under 15, against 23.3%. The post-war arrivals have aged too: 20.1% of the overseas-born were 65 or older in 2025, up from 16.0% in 1996.

Notes on data and method

Counts and estimates. Census counts (1891–1991, 2011, 2016 and 2021) record where people said they were born on Census night. Figures from 1996 onwards for Australia as a whole are estimated resident population at 30 June, which adjusts the Census for people missed or temporarily away; 2025 values are preliminary. Small differences between charts come from this, and from the ABS adding small random changes to Census cells to protect privacy.

Standardisation. Every map that compares areas of different sizes shows a rate, not a count: the share of residents born overseas (%) or speakers per 1,000 residents. Counts are shown only with symbols, dots or flows whose size is proportional to the number. Choropleths of the same measure share fixed class breaks so that the same colour means the same value everywhere: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 and 40% for the SA4 and suburb maps (Figures 9–12), and 2, 5, 10 and 20 per 1,000 for languages (Figures 18–19). Suburbs with fewer than 300 residents are greyed out because a few people can swing their share.

Geography. SA4 and SA2 boundaries are the ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard, Edition 3 (2021). Earlier Census values use the boundaries of their own year. Boundaries were simplified to keep the page small while keeping neighbouring areas aligned. Australian maps use a conic equal-area projection, so areas can be compared; world maps use Equal Earth, and Figure 5 uses an azimuthal equidistant projection centred on Sydney so that distance from Sydney reads true.

Language as a stand-in. The Census does not record a migrant’s state or province of birth, so Figures 18–19 use the language spoken at home. Speakers include Australian-born children and people born in third countries, so these figures describe communities rather than places of birth.

Colour. Sequential classes use ColorBrewer’s YlGnBu (Brewer, 2002); categories use distinct hues of similar weight; the bivariate map uses Stevens’ (2015) 3 × 3 scheme. Hover boxes and the “Show data” tables give exact values for readers who cannot rely on colour.
